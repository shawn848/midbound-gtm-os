# ABM Without Person-Level Data is Blind

Accounts don't buy. People do.

I say this a lot because it keeps being true and teams keep ignoring it.

ABM was supposed to be the upgrade from spray-and-pray outbound. And in many ways it was. Targeting specific accounts instead of blasting purchased lists is objectively smarter. Nobody argues with that.

But ABM has a blindspot that nobody wants to talk about. It operates at the account level. And accounts don't make decisions. People inside accounts make decisions. Specific people with specific roles and specific motivations who are at specific points in their buying journey.

ABM gives you the logo. It does not give you the buyer.

## The math on wasted ABM cycles

Let me walk through what a typical ABM cycle actually looks like.

Your team identifies a target account. Let's say it's Snowflake. Good fit. Right size. Right industry. Right tech stack. Snowflake goes on the list.

Now your SDR needs to find the right contact. They go to LinkedIn. They search "Snowflake" plus some title keywords. VP of Sales. Director of RevOps. Head of Marketing. They find 4 or 5 people who look like they might be involved in the buying decision.

They build a sequence. Personalized emails. LinkedIn touches. Maybe a phone call or two. They run this sequence across all 4 or 5 contacts. It takes two weeks to fully execute.

Two of those people open an email. One replies saying "not the right person, try so-and-so." The others don't respond. Your SDR follows up. Twice. Three times. Nothing.

Meanwhile, someone from Snowflake visited your website three times last week. They read your pricing page. They looked at two case studies. They checked your integration with Salesforce.

But your SDR has no idea. Because your ABM tools don't tell you who the visitor was. They might tell you "someone from Snowflake visited." Maybe. If you're running a company-level identification tool.

Even then. Snowflake has thousands of employees. "Someone from Snowflake" is about as useful as "someone on the internet."

The person who was actually on your site, showing real buying intent, never gets contacted. Your SDR is busy following up with the 4 people from LinkedIn who weren't interested.

That's ABM without person-level data. Organized guessing.

## The $30K deal that ABM missed

I'll give you a real story because I think it makes the point better than any framework.

A customer was running a POC with MidBound. They had an existing ABM program. Good team. Solid execution. Target account list. The whole setup.

One of their target accounts was a mid-market SaaS company. Their SDR had been working that account for months. Sequences on the VP of Sales and the Director of Marketing. Normal ABM playbook.

During the POC, MidBound surfaced someone from that same account on their website. An analyst. Not a VP. Not a director. An analyst.

Their team almost ignored it. Analysts weren't in their ICP targeting criteria. But the AE decided to reach out anyway because the analyst had spent time on the pricing page and a case study.

That analyst was leading the internal evaluation. The VP of Sales had delegated the entire vendor selection process. The SDR had been emailing the VP for months and the VP had been forwarding those emails to this analyst, who was doing the actual work.

The deal closed at $30K.

ABM gave them the logo. They'd had Snowflake... sorry, that account on their list for months. They knew it was a fit. They were working it.

MidBound gave them the deal. Because it surfaced the person who was actually buying.

## Where ABM stops and person-level starts

I want to be fair here. ABM isn't bad. The framework of aligning sales and marketing around target accounts is sound. It's a better approach than mass outbound.

But ABM was designed before person-level identification technology existed. It was the best you could do with the tools available. Identify accounts. Find likely contacts. Run sequences. Hope.

Person-level data fills the gap that ABM couldn't.

Here's the math.

→ ABM identifies target accounts: useful but incomplete
→ Company-level tools tell you which accounts visited your site: slightly more useful but still vague
→ Person-level identification tells you the exact individual, their role, their email, their LinkedIn, and what they looked at: actionable

The jump from step 2 to step 3 is the difference between "Snowflake is interested" and "Jordan Park, Senior Analyst at Snowflake, spent 4 minutes on your pricing page and here's how to reach them."

One of those is a talking point for your pipeline review. The other is a deal in motion.

## The buying committee problem

Modern B2B purchases involve 6 to 10 decision-makers. That number comes from Gartner and it matches what I see every day.

ABM typically targets 2 or 3 people per account. The senior titles. The obvious decision-makers. The names that show up on org charts and LinkedIn searches.

But buying committees include people you'd never target. The analyst doing the evaluation. The technical lead running the integration assessment. The operations person who'll own the tool day-to-day. The finance person who needs to approve the budget.

When MidBound shows you that three different people from the same account visited your website in the same week, you're watching a buying committee form in real time. You can see who's involved. You can see what each person cares about based on the pages they visited.

The VP looked at the executive summary. The technical lead spent time on the API docs. The analyst compared pricing tiers. Each one has a different concern. Each one needs a different conversation.

ABM gives you one sequence for the account. Person-level data gives you a tailored approach for each human.

## The speed advantage

There's a compounding benefit that gets overlooked.

When you identify a visitor in real time and reach out within hours, you're not just reaching the right person. You're reaching them at the right time. They were literally just on your website. Your product is fresh in their mind. They're actively evaluating.

Compare that to ABM's timeline. Build the account list. Research contacts. Build sequences. Execute over 2-3 weeks. Follow up for another 2 weeks. Maybe get a response.

By the time your ABM sequence reaches the right person (if it ever does), they may have already chosen a competitor. Or lost interest. Or moved to a different project.

Person-level identification compresses the entire cycle. Visitor shows intent. You identify them. You reach out. Same day. Sometimes same hour.

Speed to lead is one of the strongest predictors of win rate in B2B sales. The first vendor to have a meaningful conversation with the buyer wins a disproportionate share of deals.

## Don't abandon ABM. Upgrade it.

Don't get me wrong though. I'm not saying burn your ABM playbook. I'm saying it's incomplete without person-level data.

Keep your target account list. Keep your ICP criteria. Keep your sales and marketing alignment. All of that is valuable.

Then layer person-level identification on top. See who from those target accounts is actually on your site. See who's showing real intent. See the individuals, not just the logos.

ABM told you where to look. Person-level data tells you who to talk to.

Accounts don't buy. People do.
