# Go Vertical, Win Deeply, Then Expand

I spent a few weeks traveling between NYC, SF, and LA. 100+ meetings with VCs, angels, and founders.

Everyone was polite. Everyone asked smart questions. But the real question in every room, the one behind all the other questions, was the same.

Can this be absorbed?

Can a bigger company build this in 3 months? Can an existing player add this as a feature? Is there anything proprietary here, or is this a thin layer on top of commodity infrastructure?

If you can't answer that with something concrete, the meeting ends politely and nothing happens.

## The pattern in every "no"

The VCs who passed on us early on were consistent in their reasoning. Not that the product was bad. Not that the market was small. But that the moat wasn't clear enough yet.

"What stops Clearbit from adding person-level identification?" "What happens when 6sense decides to go downmarket?" "If ZoomInfo builds this, what's your response?"

Fair questions. Hard questions. The kind of questions that expose whether you're building a real company or a feature that'll get absorbed by a platform.

Our early answers weren't good enough. I'll admit that. We were too focused on the product and not enough on the structural advantages that would make the product defensible.

The trip changed how I think about this.

## Go vertical first

The most consistent advice from the investors who actually engaged deeply (not the ones who gave surface-level feedback) was some version of the same thing.

Go vertical. Win deeply in one segment. Become the default in that niche before you think about expanding.

This sounds obvious when you say it. It's incredibly hard to practice. Because when you have a horizontal product that could serve anyone, the temptation is to serve everyone. Cast a wide net. Take every customer that shows up. Be the platform for all B2B teams.

That temptation is a trap.

When you try to serve everyone, you serve no one deeply. Your product stays generic. Your messaging stays vague. Your case studies are all over the map. A prospect looks at your customer page and sees a logistics company, a fintech startup, and an enterprise healthcare firm and thinks "none of those are me."

When you go vertical, everything sharpens.

Your product builds features specific to that vertical. Your messaging speaks their language. Your case studies are from their peers. Your integrations connect to their tools. Your sales team understands their problems at a level that horizontal competitors can't match.

10 customers who can't live without you is worth more than 100 who kind of like you.

## Win deeply means they'd fight for you

There's a test I started using after the trip.

Would this customer be angry if we disappeared tomorrow? Not inconvenienced. Angry. Would they call us? Would they try to talk us out of shutting down? Would they consider hiring our engineers to keep the product alive?

If yes, you've won deeply. If they'd shrug and find an alternative in a week, you haven't.

The difference between these two states is enormous. And it's not just about product quality. It's about how embedded you are in their workflow.

A tool they check once a week is replaceable. A tool that sends real-time alerts to their sales team's Slack, syncs identified visitors to their HubSpot pipeline, and drives their entire speed-to-lead workflow is not replaceable. It's infrastructure.

When we started thinking about MidBound this way, it changed our product decisions. We stopped adding features that looked good in a demo and started building integrations that made us harder to rip out.

Slack alerts that SDRs rely on every morning. HubSpot syncs that power their pipeline reporting. Webhook integrations that feed their custom dashboards.

Each one of those is an embedding point. Each one makes us more deeply woven into how the team operates. Each one increases the switching cost without us ever having to lock anyone in with a contract.

Win deeply means becoming part of the infrastructure, not just another tool in the stack.

## Proprietary data is the real leverage

The meetings where investors leaned in. Where they stopped checking their phones and started asking specifics. Were the ones where we talked about data.

Proprietary data is the moat that's hardest to replicate. Not code. Code can be rebuilt. Not features. Features can be copied. Not even speed to market. Speed is temporary.

But a dataset that improves with every customer, that compounds over time, that creates a flywheel where more data makes the product better which attracts more customers which generates more data. That's structural.

For MidBound, the proprietary data advantage works like this. Every time we identify a visitor and that identification is confirmed or corrected by a customer, our matching model gets better. Every new website we're installed on broadens our understanding of visitor patterns. Every ICP configuration refines how we think about buyer personas.

This isn't a dataset you can buy from a vendor. It's not public LinkedIn data repackaged. It's a matching intelligence that gets sharper with use.

That's the answer to "what stops Clearbit from building this?" They could build the feature. They can't replicate the data flywheel. Not without a base of customers actively using person-level identification across thousands of websites.

## Velocity is a moat

The other thing I took away from the trip is that speed is underrated as a competitive advantage.

Not speed of building features. Speed of the entire cycle. Ship, learn, adjust, ship again. The team that completes this loop fastest wins.

We're a team of three. When a customer requests something, we can ship it in days. When we spot a pattern in the data, we can build a feature around it in a week. When a competitor announces something, we can respond before their feature is out of beta.

Large companies can't do this. They have product review committees. Quarterly planning cycles. Engineering sprints that are locked in months ahead. A feature request goes into a backlog, gets prioritized next quarter, gets scoped, gets pushed to the quarter after that.

We just build it.

That velocity is a moat because it means we're always closer to what customers actually need. We're not building what customers needed six months ago. We're building what they need this week.

The VCs who understood this were the ones who'd built companies themselves. They knew that velocity compounds. A team that ships 2x faster doesn't just get to market 2x sooner. They learn 2x faster. They course-correct 2x faster. Over 18 months, that compounds into a massive advantage.

## The expansion question

"Go vertical, win deeply, then expand." The third part is important. It's not "go vertical and stay there forever."

The point of going vertical first is to build the foundation that makes expansion possible. Vertical domination gives you revenue, case studies, product maturity, and a dataset that generalizes.

Once you own one vertical, the next one is easier. Your product has been hardened by real usage. Your team knows how to onboard and retain customers. Your data model has seen enough patterns to work in adjacent segments.

The mistake is expanding before you've won deeply. When you expand prematurely, you spread thin. Your resources split. Your messaging fragments. Your product accumulates features for three different verticals without being great for any of them.

I've watched startups do this. They get one or two customers in each of five verticals and call it "traction." It's not traction. It's experimentation without commitment.

Commitment to a vertical feels scary because you're saying no to potential customers. But saying no to the wrong customers is what lets you say a much more convincing yes to the right ones.

## What I brought home from the trip

The trip recalibrated how I think about building MidBound. Not what we're building. That was always clear. Person-level identification of website visitors. But how we build the company around it.

Go vertical. B2B SaaS teams with 1,000+ US monthly visitors and $3K+ ACV. Win those customers so deeply they'd be upset if we disappeared.

Build the proprietary data flywheel. Every customer makes the matching model better. Every identified visitor adds to the dataset. Every ICP configuration sharpens the intelligence.

Move fast. Stay small. Ship weekly. Be closer to the customer than any competitor can be. Let velocity compound.

Then expand. One vertical at a time. Each one built on the foundation of the one before.

That's the strategy. It's not complicated. But I've learned that the best strategies rarely are. The hard part is the discipline to execute them without getting distracted by every opportunity that walks through the door.

I should have opened a daycare in Minnesota. It would have been much simpler.

But this is more fun.
